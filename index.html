WHY THESE EXIST AT ALL

None of this is cynical. A hotel that genuinely respects every guest still has no way to prove it beyond its own word. A small property often can't absorb the cost of an elaborate audit system. A STANDARD THAT ALREADY WORKS

The restaurant industry solved a version of this a century ago.

Michelin inspectors visit anonymously. No restaurant can buy a star, and none can self-report one. The QueerTech Verifications borrows that discipline and points it at human safety.

Why anonymity is the whole mechanism.

A Michelin star works because the restaurant never knows which table is the inspector's. There's no version of the meal to perform for a judge and a different version for everyone else — so the only way to earn the star is to be that good, every night, for every table.

Self-reported badges fail for the opposite reason: the property knows exactly when it's being graded, because it's the one filling out the form. The QueerTech Verifications borrows the one design decision that makes Michelin's system hard to game — the property doesn't know when the visit is happening, and doesn't get to choose who shows up.

And accountability doesn't end at the visit. A Michelin star can be pulled the following year if standards slip. QueerTech's certification is time-stamped, geo-tagged, and re-audited on a fixed six-month cycle — verified isn't a badge earned once and kept forever. It's a standard that has to keep being met.

WHY THIS PROCESS EXISTS

We didn't design a checklist by guessing what travelers worry about. We built it by asking them — and by walking the same hotels they had. What we found wasn't one bad actor. It was a structural gap: every existing system checks a hotel once, or never, or only after someone's already been hurt. So the process below isn't a formality. Every step exists because a specific failure, from a specific traveler's story, showed us it had to.

Our Verification Process

01 // REGISTRATION. A hotel registers and submits documentation — trade licence, fire-safety certificate, staff records — before any audit is even scheduled.

Every submission is reviewed by a person, against a fixed checklist — not auto-approved, not rubber-stamped.

02 // DOCUMENT VERIFICATION. Documentation is verified, a fee is paid, and a visit is booked — the property doesn't get to pick who shows up.

Documents are checked against public records before a visit is ever scheduled — trade licence, fire-safety certificate, staff records, cross-verified, not just collected.

03 // THE SCHEDULE. A visit is booked. The auditor arrives as a guest — unannounced, unrehearsed, the way any traveler would.

04 // THE PROTOCOL. The same structured checklist, every property, every city — staff conduct, door locks, lighting, fire exits. No shortcuts, no self-grading.

The auditor moves through the property the way a guest would — reception, room, corridor, fire exit — logging exactly what a guest would notice, and a dozen things they wouldn't think to check.

05 // THE SWEEP. A dedicated, technical search for hidden recording devices — optical, radio-frequency, and non-linear-junction detection — the one check no guest can do for themselves.

Optical, RF, and non-linear-junction detection sweep every room on the visit list — the one check that requires equipment a guest could never bring themselves.

06 // THE MARK. A property earns the mark. It doesn't apply for one, complete a course, and print it out. The brass seal is geo-tagged, time-stamped, and verified live.

The mark isn't printed and handed over. It's issued live, geo-tagged and time-stamped to that specific visit — traceable back to the exact audit that earned it.

07 // THE RE-AUDIT. Verified isn't forever. Certification runs six months and is revoked the same day a confirmed complaint comes in — because trust that's never tested stops meaning anything.

Verified isn't a permanent status. Every certification carries an expiry and a re-audit date from the day it's issued — the standard has to keep being earned.

WHY WE STARTED

We were traveling for work, not looking for a cause. Somewhere between a booking confirmation and a door that didn't feel the way the listing promised, the question stopped being hypothetical. If it could happen to us — prepared, careful, paying attention — it was already happening, quietly, to people with far less room to be careful than we had.

We went looking for the number. Not a hunch — the actual number. What we found was worse than one bad night: a pattern, repeated across cities, hiding in plain sight behind five-star ratings and inclusion badges nobody had ever walked in to check. So we didn't write a complaint. We built a checklist. QueerTech exists because a promise without an inspection is just a nicer word for a guess — and travelers deserve better than a guess.
