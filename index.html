STORY MODE — THREE ARRIVALS

Three travelers. Three doors.
You decide if either felt safe.

1 / 6

01 // MEERA. Meera's booked eleven cities this year. She knows what a good room looks like. Five stars. Forty reviews. A lobby that smells expensive. She stops double-checking. Almost. The smoke detector sits half a centimetre off-true. Probably nothing. She unscrews it anyway. Finds nothing. Feels nothing like relief. 'Verified' was a word on a website. Tonight, it isn't a word she trusts.

02 // ALEX. Alex booked a double. The confirmation said double. At the desk, the smile changes when it reaches their partner's hand in theirs. 'A twin's all we have tonight,' the manager says. The system shows six doubles free. No one raises their voice. No one has to. They keep the confirmation email open the whole stay — in case someone asks for proof they belong here.

03 // VARUN. Varun picked this one for the badge on the listing: Safety Verified. Verified by whom, exactly? He didn't check. The badge did the checking for him. Midnight — the fire exit is a storage room. The extinguisher expired two winters ago. He calls the front desk. It rings out. He calls again. The badge is still on the listing the next morning. Nothing about it has changed. Neither has the extinguisher.

Just like them. Not a hunch — a pattern, repeated across cities, happening to people who had far less room to be careful than they did.

The broken contract between travelers and the places they trust.

On one side, the guest — paying for safety they cannot verify. On the other, the industry — selling trust it cannot prove. The gap between the two is where every story above lives.

SELF-ATTACHED BADGES

A property completes training, checks its own boxes, and receives a mark of inclusion. No one from outside ever walks the building.

AFTER-THE-FACT REVIEWS

Honest, but retroactive. By the time a review warns you, someone already lived the warning.

ONE-TIME AUDITS

A single visit, a checklist ticked, and a certificate printed. No follow-up. No accountability. The certificate ages, but the sticker stays.

FAILED TRAINING PROGRAMS

A projector in an empty room. A checkbox marked complete. The training happened — it just didn't change anything.

A badge is a promise. A promise isn't an inspection. None of this is malice. Most of it is good intentions without a mechanism. Trust needs a mechanism.

A STANDARD THAT ALREADY WORKS

The restaurant industry solved a version of this a century ago.

Michelin inspectors visit anonymously. No restaurant can buy a star, and none can self-report one. The QueerTech Verifications borrows that discipline and points it at human safety.

Our Verification Process

01 // REGISTRATION. A hotel registers and submits documentation — trade licence, fire-safety certificate, staff records — before any audit is even scheduled.

VERIFICATION IN ACTION

02 // DOCUMENT VERIFICATION. Documentation is verified, a fee is paid, and a visit is booked — the property doesn't get to pick who shows up.

THE REGISTRATION PROCESS

03 // THE SCHEDULE. A visit is booked. The auditor arrives as a guest — unannounced, unrehearsed, the way any traveler would.

04 // THE PROTOCOL. The same structured checklist, every property, every city — staff conduct, door locks, lighting, fire exits. No shortcuts, no self-grading.

THE AUDIT IN PROGRESS

05 // THE SWEEP. A dedicated, technical search for hidden recording devices — optical, radio-frequency, and non-linear-junction detection — the one check no guest can do for themselves.

HIDDEN CAMERA DETECTION

06 // THE MARK. A property earns the mark. It doesn't apply for one, complete a course, and print it out. The brass seal is geo-tagged, time-stamped, and verified live.

CERTIFICATION AWARDED

07 // THE RE-AUDIT. Verified isn't forever. Certification runs six months and is revoked the same day a confirmed complaint comes in — because trust that's never tested stops meaning anything.

WHY WE STARTED

We were traveling for work, not looking for a cause. Somewhere between a booking confirmation and a door that didn't feel the way the listing promised, the question stopped being hypothetical. If it could happen to us — prepared, careful, paying attention — it was already happening, quietly, to people with far less room to be careful than we had.

We went looking for the number. Not a hunch — the actual number. What we found was worse than one bad night: a pattern, repeated across cities, hiding in plain sight behind five-star ratings and inclusion badges nobody had ever walked in to check. So we didn't write a complaint. We built a checklist. QueerTech exists because a promise without an inspection is just a nicer word for a guess — and travelers deserve better than a guess.

THE DHARMA OF SAFETY

Building this mattered because a door that lies to you is a small harm that repeats, silently, at scale — and most of what protects people isn't grand. It's someone who checked. A life is a short arrangement of ordinary rooms: the ones we slept in, the ones we trusted, the ones we didn't have to think about twice. That's the whole ambition here. Not a platform. Fewer rooms people have to think twice about.

We don't need people who see the money.
We need people who saw the problem before we said a word.

Building the checklist was the easy part. What we're looking for now isn't capital first — it's people who measure impact the way we do: in doors that finally deserve the trust placed in them. If that's a fund, good. If that's you, better.
